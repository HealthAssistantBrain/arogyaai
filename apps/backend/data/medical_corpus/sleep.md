# Sleep Clinical Reference

## Symptoms

Sleep problems may present as difficulty falling asleep, frequent awakenings, early morning waking, non-restorative sleep, daytime sleepiness, morning headache, irritability, poor concentration, low motivation, fatigue, or reduced exercise tolerance. Snoring, witnessed pauses in breathing, gasping, dry mouth on waking, and morning hypertension can suggest sleep-disordered breathing. Restless legs, nightmares, shift-work misalignment, pain, reflux, nocturia, anxiety, depression, medications, caffeine, alcohol, and screen timing can all contribute.

Wearable sleep duration and sleep-stage estimates can help track patterns, but they should be interpreted cautiously. Consistently short sleep, irregular schedules, and low perceived recovery may worsen cardiometabolic risk.

## Causes

Sleep disruption can be caused by stress, anxiety, depression, chronic pain, reflux, nocturia, caffeine, alcohol, nicotine, late meals, irregular schedules, shift work, medications, restless legs, sleep apnea, and an uncomfortable sleep environment. Medical contributors can include poorly controlled diabetes, respiratory disease, cardiovascular disease, thyroid disease, pregnancy, and mood disorders.

Cause assessment should look for patterns such as difficulty initiating sleep, frequent awakenings, early waking, snoring or witnessed apnea, daytime sleepiness, nocturia, pain, medication timing, and substance use.

## Risk Factors

Risk factors for insomnia include stress, anxiety, depression, chronic pain, irregular schedules, shift work, excess caffeine, alcohol use, nicotine, late heavy meals, poor sleep environment, and certain medications. Risk factors for obstructive sleep apnea include snoring, obesity or central adiposity, large neck circumference, nasal obstruction, family history, male sex, menopause, hypertension, diabetes, and resistant high blood pressure.

Sleep disruption is linked with appetite changes, insulin resistance, blood pressure elevation, lower activity, mood symptoms, and reduced medication adherence. Sleep findings should be connected to glucose trends, blood pressure, resting heart rate, activity, stress, and reported symptoms.

## Red Flags

Urgent attention is needed when sleepiness causes dangerous driving, workplace accidents, confusion, sudden weakness, chest pain, severe breathlessness at night, fainting, or suicidal thoughts. New severe headache on waking with neurological symptoms, severe depression, or abrupt behavioral changes during sleep require clinician review. A person who wakes gasping repeatedly with chest pain, blue lips, or severe shortness of breath should seek emergency care.

Very loud snoring with witnessed apneas and daytime sleepiness should prompt timely evaluation because untreated sleep apnea can worsen blood pressure, arrhythmia risk, and metabolic health. Sedative use with alcohol or breathing problems can be hazardous.

## Clinical Notes

Clinical sleep assessment includes sleep schedule, duration, sleep latency, awakenings, snoring, witnessed apnea, daytime sleepiness, mood, pain, substances, medications, work schedule, and bedroom environment. Screening tools may support evaluation, but diagnosis of sleep apnea usually requires a sleep study. Insomnia is often managed with behavioral approaches before long-term sedative medication.

Sleep complaints can be both a cause and consequence of metabolic or cardiovascular risk. Poor sleep may worsen glucose control, while nocturia, reflux, pain, anxiety, or poorly controlled diabetes may fragment sleep. The safest interpretation is bidirectional and trend-based.

## Recommendations

Basic management includes a consistent wake time, morning light exposure, regular activity, limiting late caffeine and alcohol, winding down before bed, reserving the bed for sleep and intimacy, keeping the room cool and dark, and avoiding long late naps. If awake for a prolonged period, leaving bed briefly for a quiet low-light activity can reduce conditioned wakefulness.

For suspected sleep apnea, weight management when appropriate, avoiding alcohol near bedtime, side sleeping, nasal obstruction treatment, and clinician evaluation are reasonable. Continuous positive airway pressure or other therapies require medical assessment. Persistent insomnia, severe daytime sleepiness, or red flags should lead to professional care.
