# Diabetes Clinical Reference

## Symptoms

Diabetes can present gradually, especially in type 2 diabetes, and early symptoms may be subtle. Common symptoms include increased thirst, frequent urination, increased hunger, fatigue, blurred vision, slow wound healing, recurrent skin or urinary infections, unexplained weight change, and tingling or numbness in the feet. Symptoms can fluctuate with meals, illness, sleep disruption, medication adherence, and hydration.

Symptom interpretation should be linked to fasting glucose, HbA1c, body mass index, waist circumference, blood pressure, lipid profile, activity trends, sleep quality, family history, and medication use. A single symptom rarely confirms diabetes; patterns over time and objective glucose testing are more useful.

## Causes

Diabetes develops when insulin production or insulin action is not enough for normal glucose regulation. Type 1 diabetes is usually autoimmune beta-cell loss, while type 2 diabetes commonly reflects insulin resistance combined with gradual beta-cell dysfunction. Gestational diabetes, pancreatic disease, endocrine disorders, steroid medicines, acute illness, and major stress can also raise glucose or reveal an underlying tendency.

Symptoms may worsen during infection, dehydration, missed medication, high intake of refined carbohydrates or sugary drinks, poor sleep, reduced activity, and use of medicines that increase glucose. Digital guidance should describe these as possible contributors, not as a diagnosis.

## Risk Factors

Risk increases with overweight or obesity, central adiposity, physical inactivity, age over 35, family history of type 2 diabetes, history of gestational diabetes, polycystic ovary syndrome, hypertension, dyslipidemia, smoking, obstructive sleep apnea, chronic stress, and long-term use of medications that raise glucose.

Prediabetes, elevated fasting glucose, high triglycerides, low HDL cholesterol, fatty liver disease, and repeated high random glucose readings are important early signals. Risk should be interpreted as cumulative rather than driven by BMI alone.

## Red Flags

Urgent evaluation is needed for symptoms suggesting severe hyperglycemia or diabetic ketoacidosis, including vomiting, abdominal pain, deep or rapid breathing, fruity-smelling breath, confusion, severe weakness, dehydration, or drowsiness. Very high glucose readings, especially with ketones or illness, should be treated as urgent. Sudden vision loss, chest pain, stroke-like symptoms, severe foot infection, blackened skin, or fever with a wound also require immediate medical care.

Hypoglycemia can occur in people using insulin or sulfonylureas. Sweating, trembling, palpitations, confusion, seizure, or loss of consciousness should prompt fast-acting carbohydrate if awake and emergency care if severe or persistent.

## Clinical Notes

Diagnosis is usually based on HbA1c, fasting plasma glucose, oral glucose tolerance testing, or random plasma glucose with symptoms. HbA1c can be misleading in anemia, pregnancy, recent blood loss, kidney disease, hemoglobin variants, or altered red blood cell turnover.

Diabetes care focuses on cardiovascular and kidney risk reduction as much as glucose control. Blood pressure, lipids, urine albumin, kidney function, foot sensation, retinal screening, vaccination status, and smoking history are clinically relevant. Advice should prioritize achievable behavior changes, medication review, and timely clinician follow-up.

## Recommendations

Basic management includes balanced meals with high-fiber carbohydrates, adequate protein, reduced sugary drinks, regular physical activity, weight management when appropriate, smoking cessation, sleep improvement, and stress management. Walking after meals can help reduce post-meal glucose spikes. People using glucose-lowering medication should understand hypoglycemia symptoms and sick-day guidance.

A clinician may recommend metformin or other agents based on glucose level, kidney function, cardiovascular disease, heart failure, weight goals, and cost. General guidance is to arrange confirmatory testing, review trends, monitor symptoms, and seek urgent care for red flags.
