# General Symptoms Clinical Reference

## Symptoms

General symptoms such as fever, fatigue, dizziness, headache, nausea, abdominal discomfort, body aches, weakness, appetite change, and unexplained weight change are common and nonspecific. They may reflect infection, dehydration, sleep disruption, stress, medication effects, anemia, thyroid disease, metabolic problems, cardiovascular disease, neurological disease, pregnancy, or mental health conditions. The clinical meaning depends on onset, duration, severity, progression, associated symptoms, exposures, and baseline health.

Digital assessment should avoid overfitting a single symptom to one diagnosis. A useful approach is to identify symptom clusters, time course, vital-sign changes, risk factors, recent medication changes, travel, sick contacts, and objective measurements such as temperature, blood pressure, heart rate, glucose, oxygen saturation, and lab results.

## Risk Factors

Higher-risk users include infants, older adults, pregnant people, immunocompromised people, those with diabetes, heart disease, kidney disease, chronic lung disease, cancer, recent surgery, or complex medication regimens. Dehydration risk increases with vomiting, diarrhea, fever, heat exposure, diuretics, poor intake, and uncontrolled glucose. Infection risk may rise with recent exposure, wounds, urinary symptoms, respiratory symptoms, or poor vaccination coverage.

Medication-related symptoms are common. New dizziness, fatigue, cough, swelling, bleeding, rash, or gastrointestinal symptoms should be reviewed alongside recent prescriptions, over-the-counter drugs, supplements, and missed doses.

## Red Flags

Emergency care is needed for chest pain, severe shortness of breath, fainting, new confusion, seizure, one-sided weakness, facial droop, trouble speaking, severe sudden headache, stiff neck with fever, persistent vomiting, black or bloody stools, coughing blood, severe abdominal pain, signs of shock, blue lips, severe allergic swelling, suicidal thoughts, or rapidly worsening illness.

Fever with immunosuppression, pregnancy, very young or older age, recent surgery, central line, or severe dehydration deserves urgent advice. Any symptom that feels unusually severe to the person, or is clearly worsening despite basic care, should be escalated.

## Clinical Notes

General symptom assessment should first separate urgent from non-urgent patterns. Red flags, abnormal vital signs, and high-risk background conditions take priority over diagnostic speculation. When urgent signs are absent, trend monitoring, hydration, sleep, medication review, and planned follow-up may be reasonable.

Clinical notes should be framed with uncertainty. For example, fatigue can be related to sleep debt, anemia, thyroid disease, depression, infection, diabetes, heart disease, or medication effects. The goal is to recommend the next safe step, not to claim a final diagnosis from limited data.

## Basic Management

Basic management includes rest, fluids, nutrition as tolerated, avoiding alcohol during acute illness, monitoring temperature and symptoms, reviewing recent medications, and seeking routine care if symptoms persist or recur. Users should record onset, triggers, associated symptoms, home readings, and what improves or worsens the symptom.

For mild self-limited symptoms without red flags, supportive care and observation may be appropriate. For persistent fever, unexplained weight loss, recurrent dizziness, worsening fatigue, abnormal home measurements, or symptoms affecting daily function, clinician evaluation is recommended.
