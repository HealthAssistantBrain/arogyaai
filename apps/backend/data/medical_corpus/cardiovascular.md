# Cardiovascular Clinical Reference

## Symptoms

Cardiovascular symptoms include chest pressure, tightness, heaviness, burning, shortness of breath, palpitations, dizziness, fainting, swelling of the legs, reduced exercise tolerance, fatigue, and pain radiating to the jaw, shoulder, back, or arm. Symptoms may appear during exertion, emotional stress, after heavy meals, or at rest. Some people, especially older adults and people with diabetes, may have atypical symptoms such as nausea, sweating, indigestion-like discomfort, unexplained fatigue, or breathlessness without classic chest pain.

Palpitations should be interpreted with context such as heart rate pattern, stimulant use, dehydration, anxiety, fever, anemia, medication changes, and wearable rhythm alerts. Shortness of breath may reflect cardiac, respiratory, metabolic, or anxiety-related causes, so associated signs matter.

## Causes

Cardiovascular symptoms can be caused by coronary artery disease, arrhythmia, heart failure, valve disease, high blood pressure, pulmonary embolism, anemia, thyroid disease, fever, dehydration, medication effects, stimulant use, anxiety, or respiratory illness. Acute symptoms should be triaged separately from long-term prevention because myocardial infarction, stroke, dangerous arrhythmia, and pulmonary embolism are time-sensitive.

Digital guidance should connect symptoms with risk markers and objective data, but it should not diagnose a specific cardiac condition from wearable trends or symptom text alone.

## Risk Factors

Major risk factors include hypertension, high LDL cholesterol, diabetes, smoking, chronic kidney disease, obesity, sedentary behavior, unhealthy diet, excess alcohol, sleep apnea, family history of premature cardiovascular disease, and increasing age. Inflammatory conditions, pregnancy-related hypertension, early menopause, chronic stress, and air pollution exposure can also increase risk.

Risk should be viewed as a combined profile rather than a single number. Repeated high home blood pressure readings, persistent resting tachycardia, or worsening exercise tolerance deserve clinician review.

## Red Flags

Emergency care is needed for chest pain or pressure lasting more than a few minutes, chest discomfort with sweating, nausea, fainting, severe shortness of breath, pain spreading to the arm or jaw, new weakness on one side, facial droop, trouble speaking, sudden severe headache, blue lips, collapse, or confusion. Palpitations with fainting, chest pain, severe breathlessness, or a very rapid persistent pulse are urgent.

Sudden leg swelling with chest pain or breathlessness can suggest pulmonary embolism or heart failure and should not be managed with home advice alone. Severe hypertension with chest pain, neurological symptoms, severe headache, or visual changes requires urgent medical assessment.

## Clinical Notes

Cardiovascular assessment commonly includes history, examination, blood pressure measurement, ECG, lipid profile, glucose or HbA1c, kidney function, and sometimes troponin, echocardiography, stress testing, or ambulatory rhythm monitoring. Wearable data can help identify trends but does not replace clinical evaluation, especially for acute symptoms.

Clinical reasoning should distinguish stable risk reduction from acute symptom triage. For prevention, the focus is long-term control of blood pressure, lipids, glucose, body weight, activity, and smoking. For acute symptoms, time-sensitive conditions such as myocardial infarction, stroke, pulmonary embolism, and dangerous arrhythmias must be considered.

## Recommendations

Basic management includes regular aerobic activity as tolerated, resistance training, reduced sodium intake for high blood pressure, a diet rich in vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains, nuts, and unsaturated fats, smoking cessation, weight management when appropriate, sleep apnea evaluation when suggested, and medication adherence.

Medication decisions such as statins, antihypertensives, antiplatelets, anticoagulants, or diabetes agents with cardiovascular benefit should be individualized by a clinician. General digital guidance should encourage trend review, prompt follow-up for persistent symptoms, and emergency care for red flags.
