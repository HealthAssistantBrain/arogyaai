# Respiratory Clinical Reference

## Symptoms

Respiratory symptoms include cough, wheeze, shortness of breath, chest tightness, sputum production, fever, sore throat, nasal congestion, reduced exercise tolerance, and noisy breathing. Cough may be dry or productive and can be acute after viral infection or persistent due to asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, reflux, allergies, postnasal drip, medication effects, smoking, or environmental exposure. Breathlessness should be interpreted with onset, triggers, oxygen saturation if available, fever, chest pain, heart rate, and prior lung or heart disease.

Wheezing or chest tightness after exercise, allergens, smoke, cold air, or infections may suggest airway hyperreactivity. Productive cough with fever can suggest infection, while chronic cough with weight loss, blood, or smoking history requires medical review.

## Risk Factors

Respiratory risk factors include smoking, secondhand smoke, air pollution, occupational dust or chemical exposure, asthma, chronic lung disease, recurrent infections, poor vaccination status, immunosuppression, obesity, sleep apnea, reflux, allergies, and heart failure. Recent travel, exposure to contagious illness, tuberculosis contact, or high-risk workplace exposure can change the urgency of evaluation.

Digital triage should consider baseline function. A mild symptom in a person with severe asthma, COPD, heart disease, pregnancy, older age, or immunocompromise may deserve earlier care than the same symptom in a low-risk person.

## Red Flags

Emergency care is needed for severe shortness of breath, blue lips, inability to speak full sentences, confusion, fainting, chest pain, oxygen saturation persistently below a clinician-advised threshold, coughing blood, severe allergic swelling, stridor, or rapidly worsening symptoms. Fever with stiff neck, severe dehydration, or altered mental status is urgent.

Asthma or COPD symptoms that do not improve with prescribed rescue medication, or require repeated rescue doses, need urgent assessment. Sudden breathlessness with chest pain, one-sided leg swelling, or recent surgery may suggest pulmonary embolism and should not be handled as routine cough advice.

## Clinical Notes

Respiratory assessment commonly includes symptom duration, fever pattern, exposure history, smoking status, oxygen saturation, respiratory rate, lung examination, and sometimes chest imaging, spirometry, viral testing, sputum testing, or blood work. Pulse oximeter readings can be affected by poor circulation, nail polish, movement, skin pigmentation, and device quality, so symptoms remain important.

Differentiating respiratory from cardiac causes is essential. Breathlessness with leg swelling, orthopnea, chest pressure, or rapid weight gain may reflect heart failure or ischemia. Anxiety can contribute to breathlessness, but dangerous causes should be considered first when red flags are present.

## Basic Management

Basic management for mild upper respiratory symptoms includes rest, hydration, fever control when appropriate, avoiding smoke, humidified air if helpful, and monitoring symptom progression. People with asthma or COPD should follow their written action plan and use prescribed inhalers correctly. Antibiotics are not useful for most viral illnesses and should be prescribed only when clinically indicated.

Preventive management includes smoking cessation, vaccinations when appropriate, reducing indoor pollutants, mask use during high exposure risk, and controlling allergies or reflux if they trigger cough. Persistent cough, recurrent wheeze, or worsening breathlessness should prompt clinician review.
